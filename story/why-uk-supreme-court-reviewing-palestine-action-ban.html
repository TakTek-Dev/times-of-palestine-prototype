Why has the UK Supreme Court been asked to rule on the ban on the protest group Palestine Action?

The short answer

The court heard an appeal on 4-5 November on whether the 2025 ban breaches free expression and assembly rights; judgment is still pending.

This week5 November 2026, 17:00 JerusalemLondon2 min read

Britain's Supreme Court spent 4 and 5 November hearing the final domestic appeal against the government's decision to ban Palestine Action under terrorism law. The appeal was brought by Huda Ammori, a co-founder of the direct-action group. The justices have reserved judgment, which is expected in December or January. Their ruling will settle a legal fight that has run for sixteen months and produced two contradictory decisions from the courts below.

The ban dates from July 2025. The House of Commons voted 385 to 26 on 2 July to proscribe the group, in an order that also listed two neo-Nazi organisations, and the measure took effect on 5 July under the Terrorism Act 2000. From that date, membership of the group or public expressions of support for it became criminal offences. Ammori went to the High Court the day before the ban took effect.

She won there. On 13 February 2026 three High Court judges ruled unanimously that the proscription was unlawful, both because it breached rights protected by the Human Rights Act and because the home secretary had not applied her own policy. Amnesty International and Liberty had intervened in the case, and the UN special rapporteur on counter-terrorism made submissions. The court delayed quashing the order so the government could appeal.

The government won the next round. On 15 June a five-judge Court of Appeal overturned the High Court; the lady chief justice, Baroness Carr, called the ban a justified and proportionate interference with individual rights. On 29 July the Supreme Court agreed to hear a further appeal, limited to one question: whether proscription is compatible with Articles 10 and 11 of the European Convention on Human Rights, which protect freedom of expression and of assembly.

The stakes are measured in arrests. At least 3,070 people were arrested between the ban and the end of April 2026, most for holding signs expressing support for the group at silent sit-ins in Parliament Square and Trafalgar Square, according to figures collected on the group's Wikipedia page, and more than 600 others in a later wave. The Metropolitan Police spent £8.73 million policing four days of such protests.

If Ammori succeeds, the proscription falls and the basis for the arrests made under it is called into question; if she fails, she has said she will take the case to the European Court of Human Rights in Strasbourg. Either way the judgment will define how far a British government can go in using counter-terrorism powers against a protest movement, a question that reaches well beyond campaigning on Palestine.

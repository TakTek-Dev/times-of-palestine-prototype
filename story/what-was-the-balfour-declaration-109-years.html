What was the Balfour Declaration, and why do Palestinians still mark its anniversary 109 years on?

The short answer

A 1917 letter in which Britain backed a 'national home for the Jewish people' in Palestine; Palestinians date their dispossession from it.

On record2 November 2026, 12:10 JerusalemLondon2 min read

The Balfour Declaration was a letter. On 2 November 1917 the British foreign secretary, Arthur James Balfour, wrote to Lord Rothschild, a leading figure in the Zionist movement, to say that the British government viewed with favour the establishment in Palestine of a national home for the Jewish people. The operative text was a single sentence. Monday 2 November was its 109th anniversary, which the Palestinian news agency WAFA marked with a long retrospective.

The letter followed about three years of contacts between the British government and Zionist representatives, who argued that their project could serve British strategic interests in the region. Before it was made public, London sought the approval of the US president, Woodrow Wilson. France and Italy endorsed it in 1918. At the time Palestine was still part of the Ottoman Empire, and British troops were only then advancing into it.

What turned a letter into policy was the mandate system. In 1920 the Allied powers, meeting at San Remo, awarded Britain the mandate for Palestine and charged it with putting the declaration into effect. The League of Nations approved the mandate in July 1922 and it came into force in September 1923. WAFA's account stresses that this made the declaration a commitment of the Western powers as a whole, not of Britain alone.

The text mentioned the people already living in Palestine only as existing non-Jewish communities whose civil and religious rights should not be prejudiced. It said nothing of their political rights. That omission is at the centre of the Palestinian objection: a foreign power promised a national home in a country it did not yet control to a movement based largely outside it, without consulting the population that lived there.

Opposition was immediate in the Arab world, and Britain tried to reassure Sharif Hussein of Mecca that Jewish settlement would not go beyond what was compatible with Arab interests. On the ground, immigration was facilitated under the mandate. Palestinians responded with protests and uprisings, among them the Buraq uprising of 1929 and the revolt that began in 1936. Israel was established in May 1948, and hundreds of thousands of Palestinians were displaced.

The anniversary remains a fixed date in the Palestinian calendar, marked each year by official statements and by commentary of the kind WAFA published this week. Britain's own position has shifted over the decades: it recognised the State of Palestine in September 2025. In the retrospective's closing words, the declaration was a promise made by a foreign power in a land already inhabited by another people, whose rights and aspirations were left out of the equation.

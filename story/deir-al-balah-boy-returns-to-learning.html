A 12-year-old who drove a cart to feed his family is back in class in Deir al-Balah, with help

In one line

A boy who lost his mother in a 2024 strike and left learning to work has returned to school after counselling and family support, OCHA reports.

On record4 November 2026, 10:00 JerusalemDeir al-Balah2 min read

The boy in the story is called Shadi, which is not his real name. He is 12 and comes from Deir al-Balah. In January 2024 an Israeli air strike hit his home, killing his mother, his grandmother, his aunts and other relatives and critically injuring his father, according to an account published by the UN humanitarian office OCHA on 4 November. Shadi was hurt too, and his injuries affected both his eyesight and his speech.

The family ended up at a displacement site in the same town. Relatives noticed that the boy had become quiet, withdrawn and fearful. He stopped going to learning activities and began working with an animal-drawn cart, hauling goods to bring in money. With his mother dead and his father badly injured, OCHA writes, he had taken on an adult's share of responsibility for meeting the family's basic needs at an age when he should have been in a classroom.

It was his sister who asked for help. A protection team run by DanChurchAid and its local partner, the MA'AN Development Center, assessed the household and drew up a plan for the whole family. Shadi received individual counselling and psychosocial support, and was referred for speech therapy, for help with his visual impairment and for a place in a learning programme. The family was linked to food assistance and health care to ease the financial pressure that had pushed him into work.

Over time, the account says, Shadi grew more comfortable in the sessions and started to look forward to them. He told the team that he feels very comfortable and waits for the sessions every day. With continued case management for him and his relatives, he has gone back to school. His sister said the family had learned things it did not know before, and that the support they received had changed both of them.

The project is financed by the occupied Palestinian territory Humanitarian Fund and serves more than 15,000 people in Khan Younis and Deir al-Balah through community-based counselling, psychosocial support and referrals. OCHA sets Shadi's case against wider figures: tens of thousands of children in Gaza have been injured over the past three years, UNICEF estimates that tens of thousands have lost one or both parents, and about 60 per cent of school-age children had no in-person learning in the 2024-2025 school year.

Getting children back into class is a system-wide effort. In the week to 2 November, education partners sent 164,530 school bags to 385 learning spaces, OCHA reported. The storm that followed damaged 20 of those spaces and pushed attendance at others as low as 3 per cent. The agency's authors are careful about what protection work can do: it cannot undo a child's losses, they write, but it can restore routine and a way back to learning.

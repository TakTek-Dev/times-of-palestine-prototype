JerusalemSettlements

What is the E1 plan, and why did a Bedouin hamlet's demolition raise alarm that it has begun?

The short answer

E1 is a planned settlement of 3,401 homes between Jerusalem and Ma'ale Adumim; Palestinians say demolitions at Bir al-Maskub mark its start.

This week5 November 2026, 21:09 JerusalemEast of Jerusalem2 min read

E1 is the name of a tract of hills between East Jerusalem and the large settlement of Ma'ale Adumim. Israel has had plans to build there for decades and repeatedly shelved them under international pressure, because construction would close the last open corridor linking the northern and southern West Bank past Jerusalem. Two tenders are now on the books for a total of 3,401 housing units, according to the Palestinian Authority's Wall and Settlement Resistance Commission.

The larger tender, No. 460/2025, covers 2,167 units; a second, No. 186/2026, covers 1,234. On 5 November the timetable for the first was revised: publication of the tender documents and the opening of bidding moved from 25 October to 1 December, and the closing date from 21 to 28 December. The commission stressed that this is a change of schedule only, not a cancellation or a reduction in the number of homes.

On the same day the Palestinian foreign ministry said Israeli forces had demolished about half of the structures of Bir al-Maskub, a Bedouin community inside the planning area, including four residential clusters, and handed out demolition notices for everything that remained. The ministry called it a shift from planning and paperwork to implementation on the ground, and warned that other Bedouin communities in the area could be next.

The ministry's argument is that E1 would cut the West Bank in two, separate East Jerusalem from its Palestinian surroundings and so make a contiguous Palestinian state impossible. It accused Israel's caretaker government of using the transition after the 27 October election to create facts on the ground. It called on other governments, and on the United States in particular, to act to stop the demolitions before they spread to neighbouring communities.

Foreign governments have gone beyond statements. Britain's foreign secretary, Ed Miliband, said on 18 October that the tender was a direct threat to a two-state solution and addressed companies directly: the UK, he said, has created powers to sanction individuals and firms involved in building settlements such as E1, and bidders should expect legal and reputational consequences. He also repeated a commitment to legislate against imports of settlement goods.

Bir al-Maskub is one of several herding communities around the Jerusalem to Jericho road, of which Khan al-Ahmar is the best known. On 8 November settlers from a nearby outpost stoned homes in another Bedouin community close to Khan al-Ahmar before residents drove them off, the Jerusalem Governorate said. Israel maintains that the communities were built without permits on state land; residents say they have nowhere else to go.

'For the first time I have my own chair': three Gaza siblings born without legs receive custom aids

In one line

Anas, Mohammad and Sujood Al-Sharif received wheelchairs, writing aids and walking supports made in Gaza by the NGO EducAid with UN funding.

This week5 November 2026, 11:00 JerusalemGaza Strip2 min read

Sujood Al-Sharif is 17 and until recently had never had a wheelchair that was hers. She and her brothers Mohammad, 15, and Anas, 10, were all born without legs and with two fingers on each hand. A story published by the UN humanitarian office OCHA on 5 November describes how the three received equipment designed around their bodies and their school day, paid for by the occupied Palestinian territory Humanitarian Fund and delivered by the international organisation EducAid.

For Sujood the package included a size-40 wheelchair with a detachable dining table, an adjustable slanted writing table, a waterproof shield for the lower body and hand supports for walking. She told the team that she sleeps in the chair so that her siblings cannot take it, and that it made her feel somebody cares about her. Writing used to exhaust her because she had to lie down to do it. She says she wants to study media.

Anas, the youngest, had his existing wheelchair serviced and upgraded, and was given a customised pen grip. Before, the pen kept slipping out of his hand, he moved mostly by crawling on his hands, and his mother often had to carry him. His mother told OCHA the difference was huge, particularly for writing and eating. EducAid began supporting him in 2025, while the war was still under way, according to the account.

Mohammad's aids were built after staff assessed what he needed both at school and at home. EducAid's own workshops produced the devices, among them a small skateboard-style seat that he enjoys using to get around, hand grips that his mother says protect him from wounds and slipping, and a commode chair that she said changed daily life. Mohammad's own verdict was practical: with the table and the shield, his clothes no longer get dirty.

A teacher quoted in the story said the three stand out in class, are eager to learn and had started to write more effectively. The article does not say where in Gaza the family lives; Sujood mentions leaving her tent, which suggests they are among the displaced. A separate OCHA report on the 6 to 8 November storm found that flooding hit two sites for people with disabilities, affecting 200 families, and described such households as facing heightened risk.

The Humanitarian Fund is a pooled fund managed by OCHA that channels donor money to aid organisations working in Gaza and the West Bank. The UN's news service carried the siblings' story on 7 November. Aid agencies estimate that thousands of children in Gaza now live with life-changing injuries, on top of those, like the Al-Sharif children, whose disabilities date from birth and whose equipment was lost, worn out or never available.
